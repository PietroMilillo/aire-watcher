# Researching document requirements, live

This is the step the original tool didn't have. Do it early — before any
code changes — so the person has it in hand with time to actually act on it
(get something signed, apostilled, translated) before a slot opens.

## Why this has to be researched per case, not templated

Requirements vary along two axes at once: **by service** (registering a
birth is not the same paperwork as renewing a passport) and **by
consulate** (two Italian consulates in two different countries can list
different requirements for a service with the same name, because local law
and local practice both feed into what the consulate asks for). Never reuse
a checklist from a different person's case, even one for the "same"
service — always research this specific consulate plus this specific
service.

## Search strategy

1. Start with the consulate's own site. Every Italian consulate abroad has
   its own page under the `esteri.it` network (e.g.
   `conshouston.esteri.it`) — search for the consulate name plus the exact
   service name plus "documenti necessari" (or "required documents" if the
   consulate publishes an English page too). This is the authoritative
   source; prefer it over everything else.
2. Check whether Prenot@mi itself shows a description or a linked info
   sheet for that service when logged in — some consulates attach one
   directly to the booking row. If the person is already logged in for the
   setup-walkthrough step, look there first.
3. Use general web search as corroboration, not as the primary source.
   Consulate pages are sometimes terse about details that matter in
   practice — forums and expat blogs are often where you learn that a form
   must be signed *in front of consulate staff*, not just signed and
   brought in, or that a translation must be "asseverata" (sworn) rather
   than just accurate. Treat these as leads to verify against the official
   page, not as facts to repeat unverified.

## What to flag, not smooth over

If two sources disagree — an official page says one thing and a recent
forum post from someone who just went through it says another — **say so,
explicitly, rather than picking the one that sounds more authoritative.**
The disagreement itself is useful information (consulate pages go stale;
practice on the ground sometimes differs from what's published), and the
person booking the appointment is the one who has to live with being wrong.

## Common document categories to watch for

Not a checklist to apply blindly — a prompt for what to go looking for,
since official pages don't always spell out every item:

- Valid passport / ID, and *codice fiscale* if they have one
- Proof of residence abroad
- Civil-status documents relevant to the service (birth, marriage, death
  certificates) — these frequently need an **apostille** from the issuing
  country and a **certified ("asseverata") translation into Italian**, and
  that chain takes real time to assemble
- Consulate-specific forms that must be printed, filled by hand, and
  **physically signed** — this is usually the detail someone is actually
  asking about when they ask "what do I need to have signed"
- Photographs meeting specific size/background requirements
- Reference numbers from any prior correspondence with the consulate

## Presenting the result

Give the person a checklist, not a wall of prose. For each item, note:

- Whether it needs a signature, and whose (theirs, a witness's, the
  consulate's)
- Whether it needs an apostille or certified translation
- Where you confirmed it — the exact page/URL for official items, or "only
  seen on [source], not corroborated on the official page — verify before
  relying on this" for anything less solid

That last column is the point: a confident-sounding checklist that's wrong
in one place is worse than a shorter one that's honest about its gaps.
